OLD ABSTRACT (structured version, as uploaded to Overleaf on 29 October 2026)

Background. Radiology reports describe the location of findings through language, using anatomical terms or slice numbers, but do not record their 3D extent, which leaves referring physicians to search the scan for each finding again. Segmenting each reported finding in 3D would make reports easier to communicate and visualize, allow findings to be measured and tracked over time, and let AI systems generate reports whose findings are grounded in the volume and verifiable. However, no benchmark existed for localizing free-text findings in volumetric CT.
 Methods. Given a non-contrast chest CT scan from CT-RATE and a free-text finding, participants produced a 3D segmentation of that finding. Teams trained on 2,992 scans and validated on 200 scans from the ReXGroundingCT dataset, and were evaluated on a held-out test set of 300 scans with 582 findings in 13 categories, exhaustively annotated by board-certified radiologists. The primary metric was the mean Dice per finding per case, with hit rate (Dice ≥ 0.1) and instance F1 reported as secondary metrics. We organized the ReXGrounding Challenge as part of MICCAI 2026.
 Results. The best method reached a Dice of 0.375 and a hit rate of 0.77, matching agreement between two independent radiologist groups (Dice 0.32, hit rate 0.76, on a 50-scan subset) and exceeding the best pre-challenge model (Dice 0.285). Five of the six best teams fine-tuned VoxTell, a pretrained text-promptable segmentation model, and several restricted their masks to the lobe or side named in the sentence. Non-focal findings remained far harder to segment than focal ones (Dice 0.18–0.24 vs. 0.35–0.38 among the top six teams), instance F1 stayed below inter-reader agreement (0.34 vs. 0.47), and qualitative failure analysis showed models segmenting the right abnormality in the wrong location. In total, 48 teams from 15 countries made 760 submissions to the challenge.
 Conclusions. Top methods reach Dice values in the range of agreement between independent radiologist groups, but non-focal findings and adherence to the anatomical location described in the text remain open problems. The test set remains available for benchmarking through a public leaderboard.


CURRENT ABSTRACT (31 October 2026, after shortening)

Linking each finding in a radiology report to its location in the scan would let findings be visualized, measured, tracked and verified, but no challenge had yet brought the community together to advance this task. We organized the ReXGrounding Challenge as part of MICCAI 2026. Given a non-contrast chest CT scan and a free-text finding, participants produced a 3D segmentation of that finding. The challenge used 2,992 training and 200 validation scans from the ReXGroundingCT dataset, and evaluated methods on a held-out test set of 300 scans with 582 findings in 13 categories, exhaustively annotated by board-certified radiologists. In total, 48 teams from 15 countries made 760 submissions. The best method reached a Dice of 0.375 and a hit rate of 0.77, matching agreement between two independent radiologist groups (Dice 0.32, hit rate 0.76, on a 50-scan subset). On the 100 scans of the pre-challenge benchmark, the winner reached a Dice of 0.390, against 0.285 for the best model before the challenge. Five of the six best teams fine-tuned VoxTell, a pretrained text-promptable segmentation model, and several restricted their masks to the lobe or side named in the sentence. Non-focal findings remained far harder to segment than focal ones (Dice 0.18–0.24 vs. 0.35–0.38 among the top six teams), instance F1 stayed below inter-reader agreement (0.34 vs. 0.47), and qualitative failure analysis showed models segmenting the right abnormality in the wrong location. Top methods thus reach Dice values in the range of agreement between independent radiologist groups, but non-focal findings and adherence to the anatomical location described in the text remain open problems.
